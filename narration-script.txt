c01s03 Chapter 1: Motivation concerns direction and persistence.
Goal: an outcome an action is directed toward.

Motivation is not directly visible in one action. Leaving a meeting may reflect competing goals, limited resources or a task already completed.

Describe the behaviour first. Then ask which motives are plausible and what information would distinguish them. A strong explanation makes predictions rather than attaching a character label.

c01s03b Chapter 1: People manage competing demands.
Opportunity cost: what is forgone by choosing an option. Constraint: condition limiting available action. Persistence: continuing an action over time.

Effort on one goal can reduce time for another. The available options depend on schedules, health, access and responsibilities.

Low participation in a particular setting does not imply a lack of valued goals. Ask what the person is trying to do and what the setting makes possible.

c01s04 Chapter 1: Use several forms of evidence.
Self-report: a person’s account of their experience. Behavioural measure: recorded action under defined conditions. Causal inference: a claim about what produces an effect.

A respectful conversation can clarify priorities, while behaviour over suitable opportunities can test a prediction. Neither is a complete mind-reading instrument.

Changing access or instructions may alter participation without changing an underlying preference. Measure the intended outcome and consider alternatives.

c01s05 Chapter 1: What does the evidence allow?
Motivation research uses several theoretical frameworks and measures. Findings depend on tasks, populations and how effort or persistence is defined.

A fictional story can clarify distinctions, but does not establish an individual’s true motive.

c02s03 Chapter 2: Regulation can motivate action.
Homeostasis: regulation maintaining conditions within functional ranges. Drive: motivational state associated with a biological need. Feedback: information influencing ongoing regulation.

Bodily systems regulate conditions such as energy and fluid balance through interacting signals. A physiological need can influence attention and action.

Human eating and other behaviours are not simple readings of one internal meter. Learning, availability, culture and health also contribute.

c02s03b Chapter 2: An incentive is a feature of the situation.
Incentive: anticipated outcome that can attract or deter action. Reward learning: learning relations involving valued outcomes.

A cue associated with a valued outcome can attract attention and action. Its influence depends on learning history and present circumstances.

A smell can matter without proving a biological deficit. Conversely, an environmental cue does not mean bodily needs are irrelevant.

c02s04 Chapter 2: Useful explanations include context.
Satiety: processes reducing the tendency to continue eating. Individual variation: differences in responses and circumstances. Biopsychosocial account: biological, psychological and social contributions.

Access to food, work schedules and cultural practices affect choices. Individual physiology and medical conditions can matter too.

Use the concepts to understand complexity rather than police another person’s eating. The course does not provide weight-control advice or assess disordered eating.

c02s05 Chapter 2: What does the evidence allow?
Drive and incentive accounts capture different influences. Laboratory manipulations isolate selected conditions and do not describe every real-world eating decision.

Physiological regulation, learned cues and social circumstances can all contribute; one observed choice cannot establish their relative weights.

c03s03 Chapter 3: Wanting and expecting differ.
Subjective value: importance or usefulness of an outcome. Cost: effort, time or other burden of pursuing an option.

A person can value a goal while doubting a route to it. Effort can also depend on the costs and alternatives.

Jo’s avoidance could reflect low expectancy, public pressure or unclear instruction. Ask about the particular task rather than inferring a global lack of interest.

c03s03b Chapter 3: Self-efficacy is task-specific.
Self-efficacy: belief in capability to perform a specified action. Mastery experience: successful performance relevant to capability beliefs. Calibration: match between a judgment and suitable evidence.

Confidence about one action need not extend to another. An achievable, informative practice attempt can provide evidence about what is possible.

Praise alone may not clarify capability. Compare a prediction with actual performance, then identify the missing skill or support rather than demanding confidence.

c03s04 Chapter 3: Changing the route can change the choice.
Attribution: an explanation for an outcome. Controllability: whether a factor can be influenced. Task support: assistance addressing a relevant demand.

An outcome may be explained by task design, strategy, preparation or access as well as ability. Some conditions can be changed by the person, others need external support.

Offer a clearer example or a lower-pressure rehearsal, then check what changes. Avoid replacing every structural barrier with an attitude lesson.

c03s05 Chapter 3: What does the evidence allow?
Expectancy-value frameworks explain choices using beliefs, values and costs, but measures and predictive relations vary across tasks and contexts.

A plausible motivational explanation is not a diagnosis. Beliefs and opportunities can affect each other over time.

c04s03 Chapter 4: Self-determination theory names three needs.
Relatedness: experience of connection and belonging.

Self-determination theory examines how social conditions support motivation and wellbeing. Autonomy concerns acting with a sense of volition, not necessarily acting alone.

A person can freely choose cooperation and guidance. Competence support and respectful relationships can matter alongside choice.

c04s03b Chapter 4: Structure can support autonomy.
Autonomy support: acknowledging perspective and meaningful choice. Structure: clear expectations, guidance and feedback. Control: pressure to act in a prescribed way.

Explain the purpose of a task, offer real options where possible and provide clear guidance. A safety boundary can coexist with meaningful participation.

A choice between two unusable options is not much support. Nor does vague freedom necessarily provide the resources needed to succeed.

c04s04 Chapter 4: Motivation varies in its regulation.
Intrinsic motivation: acting for interest in the activity. Extrinsic motivation: acting for a separable outcome. Internalization: taking in and endorsing a value or regulation.

Extrinsic motives vary: a person may act under pressure or because they endorse the purpose. Intrinsic enjoyment is not required for every worthwhile task.

Reward effects depend on form, expectation and context. Avoid the blanket claim that every reward destroys motivation or that every incentive helps.

c04s05 Chapter 4: What does the evidence allow?
Self-determination theory has a substantial research literature, with findings depending on measures, context and study design. Its constructs are theoretical tools, not a personality test.

Reward effects are conditional. An observational association between support and engagement does not isolate causation by itself.

c05s03 Chapter 5: A target directs attention.
Goal specificity: how clearly a desired outcome is defined. Goal difficulty: the demand relative to current capability. Feedback: information about progress or performance.

Specific, challenging goals can focus effort when commitment, knowledge and resources support them. They can also narrow attention to the counted outcome.

For unfamiliar complex tasks, a learning goal may help more than a demanding output quota. Clarify the skill and safe standard before increasing pressure.

c05s03b Chapter 5: A process plan connects cue and action.
Implementation intention: if-then plan linking a situation and action. Monitoring: checking progress against a suitable standard.

A process goal might require checking the fault list before closing a repair. An if-then plan specifies when the step should occur.

A plan must remain usable under real constraints. Check accuracy and burden rather than treating adherence to the plan as the only outcome.

c05s04 Chapter 5: Revision can be intelligent regulation.
Goal disengagement: reducing commitment to an unattainable or unsuitable goal. Re-engagement: committing to another meaningful goal. Proxy: a measure standing in for an intended outcome.

Persistence is useful only relative to a worthwhile, feasible goal. Evidence of harm, changed conditions or unattainability can justify revision.

A higher repair count with more faults may be a worse outcome. Review accuracy, safety and participant experience alongside speed.

c05s05 Chapter 5: What does the evidence allow?
Goal-setting effects depend on task knowledge, feedback, resources and the way goals are framed. A useful average effect does not justify every quota.

Output measures can create unintended trade-offs. A complete evaluation includes quality and unwanted effects.

c06s03 Chapter 6: The same option can attract and deter.
Approach motivation: movement toward a desired outcome. Avoidance motivation: movement away from an undesired outcome. Approach–avoidance conflict: one option has attractive and aversive aspects.

A presentation can offer contribution and learning while carrying social risk. Conflicting motives are not necessarily inconsistency or dishonesty.

Specify the desired and undesired outcomes. A person may need a different route to the valued goal rather than stronger pressure to choose.

c06s03b Chapter 6: Avoidance has a function and a cost.
Avoidance learning: learning actions that prevent an aversive outcome. Negative reinforcement: removal or prevention increasing an action. Short-term relief: immediate reduction in discomfort.

Avoidance can protect from real danger. In some contexts, immediate relief can also maintain avoidance that limits valued activity.

The function depends on whether the threat is real, the available alternatives and the person’s circumstances. A course exercise should not ask someone to confront danger or treat anxiety without professional support.

c06s04 Chapter 6: Change the choice architecture respectfully.
Ambivalence: mixed evaluations of an option. Opportunity: a feasible route to action. Agency: capacity for meaningful participation and choice.

Do not treat every reluctance as a symptom. Evaluate access, safety and preference before interpreting the motivational pattern.

c06s05 Chapter 6: What does the evidence allow?
Approach and avoidance frameworks describe motivational relations whose value depends on context. Immediate relief does not by itself establish a disorder or a maladaptive pattern.

Clinical assessment is needed for persistent distress or impairment; the course is conceptual education.

c07s03 Chapter 7: Feeling and expression are different measures.
Subjective feeling: the experienced quality of an emotional state. Expression: observable movement or communication associated with emotion.

Emotion can involve bodily changes, action tendencies, appraisals, expressions and subjective experience. The components need not align perfectly.

A smile can communicate reassurance, politeness or amusement. Ask what the person reports instead of treating a facial cue as a complete emotional reading.

c07s03b Chapter 7: Theories explain relationships differently.
James–Lange account: feeling involves perception of bodily changes. Cannon–Bard account: experience and bodily response occur in parallel. Two-factor account: arousal and contextual interpretation contribute to emotion.

Classic theories differ on the role and ordering of bodily response and experience. Contemporary approaches examine more complex interactions and multiple timescales.

Use the theories as testable explanations, not as three complete recipes that settle every emotion. The café story alone cannot distinguish their predictions.

c07s04 Chapter 7: Appraisal links events with concerns.
Appraisal: evaluation of significance for goals and wellbeing.

An event can feel different depending on its meaning for the person. Being watched may mean welcome recognition or threat of criticism.

Valence and arousal describe dimensions without explaining the whole cause. Emotional vocabulary should clarify a report rather than override the person’s account.

c07s05 Chapter 7: What does the evidence allow?
Emotion theories differ in constructs and predictions, and there is no universal one-to-one map between a facial movement, brain region or physiological signal and every emotion.

Measures should be interpreted together with context, individual report and their own limitations.

c08s03 Chapter 8: Regulation can occur at several points.
Emotion regulation: processes influencing which emotions occur and how they unfold. Situation selection: choosing which situations to enter. Situation modification: changing relevant features of a situation.

Regulation can involve changing a situation, directing attention, changing interpretation or altering a response. It need not mean suppressing every feeling.

A quieter room may reduce an avoidable demand. Leaving an unsafe setting may be more appropriate than reinterpreting it positively.

c08s03b Chapter 8: Reappraisal and suppression do different work.
Cognitive reappraisal: changing an interpretation to alter an emotional response. Expressive suppression: inhibiting outward emotional expression. Regulatory flexibility: adjusting strategies to goals and conditions.

Reappraisal may change the meaning of an ambiguous event. Suppression changes visible expression without necessarily resolving the feeling or situation.

Neither strategy is universally good or bad. Safety, culture, goals and timing affect usefulness. Forced positivity can obscure a real problem that needs action.

c08s04 Chapter 8: Evaluate outcomes, not strategy slogans.
Problem solving: acting on a changeable source of difficulty. Regulation goal: the intended outcome of a strategy.

Sometimes a practical change is possible; sometimes allowing a feeling while choosing an action is more useful. Acceptance does not require tolerating abuse or abandoning appropriate care.

Define the goal and check effects on functioning and relationships. Persistent distress warrants professional support rather than escalating self-directed exercises.

c08s05 Chapter 8: What does the evidence allow?
Emotion-regulation findings depend on strategy, context, culture and outcome. Associations with wellbeing do not imply that one strategy is best for every person.

This course introduces concepts, not individualized psychological treatment. Do not use regulation language to dismiss another person’s concern.

c09s03 Chapter 9: Social emotions concern relationships and standards.
Guilt: negative evaluation of a particular action. Shame: negative evaluation focused more broadly on the self. Pride: positive evaluation linked to valued achievement or identity.

The distinction between evaluating an action and condemning the whole self can clarify a response to mistakes. Experiences vary and the words do not map perfectly across languages.

An apology can support repair, but visible regret is not a unique measure of responsibility. Look at the response, context and subsequent action.

c09s03b Chapter 9: Expression follows social conditions.
Display rule: social expectation about emotional expression. Cultural context: shared practices and meanings in a setting. Emotion norm: expectation about appropriate feeling or response.

People learn expectations about when and how to express feelings. These vary within as well as across cultural groups.

Quietness may reflect respect, discomfort or concentration. Ask rather than using nationality, gender or a single expression as an emotional explanation.

c09s04 Chapter 9: Empathy does not guarantee accuracy.
Affective empathy: sharing or resonating with another’s feeling. Perspective taking: considering another’s viewpoint. Compassion: concern for another’s difficulty and wish to help.

Feeling with someone, understanding their viewpoint and wanting to help are distinct processes. Strong emotional resonance can still misread what support is wanted.

Check your interpretation and offer a concrete choice. A respectful question can be more accurate than confidence that you already know the feeling.

c09s05 Chapter 9: What does the evidence allow?
Emotion research examines social norms, appraisals and relationships using varied measures. Broad cultural averages are not fixed rules for each group member.

No expression, word or apology uniquely establishes a person’s moral character or emotional history.

c10s03 Chapter 10: Wellbeing has several meanings.
Subjective wellbeing: evaluations and experiences of life quality. Positive affect: pleasant emotional experience.

Feeling good, functioning well and finding meaning are related but distinct outcomes. A useful activity need not feel pleasant at every moment.

Ask what the workshop promises to improve and how it measures that outcome. A temporary mood change is not proof of durable wellbeing.

c10s03b Chapter 10: A testimonial leaves alternatives open.
Comparison group: a reference condition for evaluating change. Expectancy effect: influence of anticipated benefit.

Participants may improve because of attention, social contact, expectations or other changes. A credible comparison and suitable follow-up help evaluate the particular method.

Include unwanted effects and who was not helped. A technique should earn support for a defined use rather than claim universal transformation.

c10s04 Chapter 10: Make a modest, revisable recommendation.
Agency: meaningful choice in an action. Proportionate claim: conclusion matching the strength of evidence. Professional support: qualified care matched to relevant needs.

Use clear goals, accessible routes and respectful relationships. For a harmless self-chosen routine, test a manageable change and review the outcome.

Do not treat serious distress as a motivation failure. Professional help may be appropriate, and a course is not treatment or a requirement to be happy.

c10s05 Chapter 10: What does the evidence allow?
Motivation and emotion research provides concepts and conditional findings, not a universal formula for happiness. Product claims require their own evaluation.

Course visits, confidence and enthusiasm do not certify mastery or prove a wellbeing benefit.
